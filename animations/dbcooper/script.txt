On the night before Thanksgiving, nineteen seventy one, a man in a business suit jumped out of a passenger jet.
It was dark. It was raining. The plane was flying at about two hundred miles an hour.
Strapped to his body was two hundred thousand dollars in cash.
He was never seen again.
Not his body. Not his parachute. Not his real name.
The FBI chased him for forty five years, and then they gave up.
Today, more than half a century later, he is still the only unsolved hijacking in American airline history.
They call him D. B. Cooper.
But that isn't even his name.
This is everything we know, everything we found, and the clues that still don't make sense.
Part one. The man in seat eighteen C.
It's November twenty fourth, nineteen seventy one. Portland, Oregon.
At the airport, a quiet man walks up to the Northwest Orient counter.
He buys a one way ticket to Seattle. A thirty minute flight. It costs eighteen dollars and fifty two cents.
He gives his name as Dan Cooper.
Witnesses later describe him as a man in his mid forties, about six feet tall, with dark hair and brown eyes.
He wears a dark suit, a white shirt, a black clip on tie, and a raincoat.
He carries a black briefcase.
Nobody checks it. In nineteen seventy one, nobody checks anything.
There are no metal detectors. No bag scanners. You can walk onto a plane almost as easily as you get on a bus.
Flight three oh five is a Boeing seven twenty seven.
Thirty six passengers. Six crew.
Cooper takes a seat near the back, row eighteen.
He lights a cigarette, and orders a bourbon and soda.
At two fifty in the afternoon, the plane takes off.
And a few minutes later, Cooper hands a flight attendant a note.
Part two. The note.
The flight attendant's name is Florence Schaffner.
She's used to passengers slipping her notes. Usually it's a phone number.
So she drops it into her purse without reading it.
Cooper leans over and says, quietly, Miss, you'd better look at that note. I have a bomb.
She opens it.
The note says he has a bomb in his briefcase, and that she should sit next to him.
She does.
Cooper opens the briefcase, just a crack.
Inside, she sees red sticks, wires, and a battery.
Then he tells her what he wants.
Two hundred thousand dollars, in twenty dollar bills.
Four parachutes. Two main ones, and two reserves.
And a fuel truck waiting in Seattle when they land.
She carries the message to the cockpit.
The captain radios the ground, and within minutes, the FBI, the airline, and the police all know.
The passengers have no idea.
They're told there's a small mechanical problem, and the plane will circle for a while before landing.
For about two hours, Flight three oh five loops over Puget Sound.
Down on the ground, everyone scrambles.
The airline's president gives a clear order. Give him what he wants. Nobody gets hurt.
Part three. The calmest hijacker in history.
Here's the strange part.
Everyone who met Cooper that day says the same thing.
He was calm. He was polite. He was almost friendly.
He wasn't nervous. He didn't shout. He didn't wave the bomb around.
At one point he looks out the window and recognizes the city below.
He says, that's Tacoma down there.
He seems to know the area.
He even mentions McChord Air Force Base, and says it's about twenty minutes from the airport.
Meanwhile, the FBI gathers the money.
Ten thousand twenty dollar bills.

But one of the reserve parachutes is a dummy. It's sewn shut, and only meant for classroom training.
Nobody notices.
At around five forty, the plane finally lands in Seattle.
Part four. The trade.
Cooper is careful.
He tells the pilots to park the plane in a far corner of the runway, away from the terminal.
He asks for the cabin lights to be dimmed, so snipers can't see inside.
An airline worker walks out alone, carrying a heavy cloth bag full of cash.
The money weighs about twenty one pounds.
Then come the parachutes.
Once Cooper has everything, he keeps his word.
He lets all thirty six passengers go.
He lets Florence go, and the second flight attendant too.
Four crew members stay on board. The captain, the co pilot, the flight engineer, and a young flight attendant named Tina Mucklow.
Then Cooper gives the pilots their new orders.
And this is where it gets interesting.
He wants to fly to Mexico City, with a stop in Reno to refuel.
But he doesn't want to fly like a normal jet.
He wants the plane to stay low, under ten thousand feet.
He wants it to fly slowly, at around the lowest speed it can safely fly.
He wants the landing gear down, and the wing flaps lowered.
And he wants the back door open.
That last part is the clue.
The Boeing seven twenty seven had something most jets didn't.
A set of stairs that folded down from the tail.
And you could open them in the air.
Cooper knew that.
Most people didn't.
At seven forty, the plane takes off again.
Part five. Into the dark.
Once they're in the air, Cooper sends Tina Mucklow up to the cockpit.

Probably the money bag.

No.

Part six. What he left behind.
Cooper didn't leave much.

Part seven. The search.
The FBI works out roughly where Cooper must have jumped.
Somewhere over the forests of southwest Washington, near the Lewis River.
It's wild country. Thick trees, steep hills, rivers, and lakes.
And that night, it was cold, wet, and pitch black.
Soldiers, police, and volunteers search the area for weeks.
Helicopters. Planes. Boats. People on foot.
They find nothing.
No parachute. No money. No body.
The FBI sends the list of serial numbers to banks, shops, and casinos all over the world.

Part eight. The money on the river.
It's February nineteen eighty. More than eight years after the hijacking.
An eight year old boy named Brian Ingram is on a family trip along the Columbia River.

Old, rotting bundles of cash.

Part nine. The suspects.

Part ten. The clues on the tie.
Remember the tie?
For decades, it sat in an FBI evidence box.
